Integration Testing Checklist for Reliable Automations

Use this integration testing checklist to catch data failures, protect critical workflows, and launch automations your operations team can trust daily.

A workflow can look perfect in a demo and still fail the first time a sales rep enters an unusual address, an invoice is missing a line item, or NetSuite takes too long to respond. That is why an integration testing checklist is not a technical formality. It is the process that determines whether an automation saves your team time or creates expensive cleanup work.

For operations leaders, the standard is simple: every system should receive the right data, at the right time, with a clear record of what happened when something goes wrong. Testing needs to prove that standard before a workflow touches live orders, customer records, payments, or payroll data.

What Integration Testing Actually Proves

Unit testing checks whether a small piece of code works in isolation. Integration testing checks whether the real systems work together under realistic conditions. That distinction matters when Salesforce sends a closed-won opportunity to an ERP, a document platform triggers an approval, or an AI process extracts information from an emailed PDF.

The failures are usually not dramatic at first. A field maps incorrectly. A date changes format. A workflow creates two records when a user retries an action. A third-party API rate limit stops a batch halfway through. Each one can leave the business with conflicting information across the systems people rely on to make decisions.

A good test plan does more than confirm the happy path. It shows how the automation behaves when inputs are incomplete, platforms are slow, permissions change, and users do the unpredictable things users always do.

Integration Testing Checklist Before Production

Use this integration testing checklist as a release gate, not a list your team checks after launch. The depth of testing depends on the workflow. A simple Slack alert needs less scrutiny than a process that creates invoices in QuickBooks or updates fulfillment records in NetSuite. But any workflow that moves operational or financial data needs evidence, not assumptions.

1. Confirm the business rule before testing the connection

Start with the operational outcome. Define what starts the workflow, what data must move, what result should be created, and who owns exceptions. If a Salesforce opportunity is marked closed-won, for example, does it create a customer, a sales order, a project, or all three in the downstream system? What happens if the account already exists?

Write down the expected result in business language before reviewing API calls and field mappings. Otherwise, a technically successful integration can still automate the wrong process.

2. Test field mapping with real-world records

Do not test only with clean sample data. Use a controlled set of records that represent the messiness of actual operations: long company names, blank optional fields, multiple contacts, special characters, international addresses if applicable, discount lines, tax variations, and records that already exist downstream.

Verify every required field, but also verify transformations. Currency, time zones, dates, status values, IDs, checkboxes, and picklists are common sources of silent errors. A field can arrive in the destination system and still be wrong enough to break reporting, billing, or fulfillment.

For high-value workflows, compare source and destination records side by side. Confirm that the automation preserves both meaning and ownership, not just the raw text.

3. Validate the complete workflow, not one step

An integration may involve a CRM, an approval layer, an ERP, a document generator, and notifications in Teams or Slack. Test the full sequence from the original trigger through the final outcome.

This is where timing becomes visible. A downstream record may depend on a prior update that has not finished processing. A workflow may send a notification before a transaction is fully committed. Test both normal volume and realistic bursts, such as a batch import or several reps closing deals at the end of the month.

Ask a practical question: if this ran 100 times during a busy week, would each transaction complete once, in the right order, without someone checking it manually?

4. Test failures deliberately

Failure testing is where production-grade automation separates itself from a fragile connector. Intentionally create the conditions the system will eventually face: expired credentials, an unavailable API, missing required data, invalid record formats, duplicate submissions, permission failures, and downstream timeouts.

For each failure, verify four things:

  • The workflow stops or retries in the correct way.
  • It does not create duplicate or partial records.
  • The responsible person receives a useful alert.
  • The error includes enough context to resolve the issue quickly.

An alert that says "workflow failed" is not operationally useful. A useful alert identifies the failed record, the affected system, the reason for failure, the retry status, and the next action. The person on call should not need to search five platforms to understand what happened.

5. Verify retries, idempotency, and recovery

Most integration failures are temporary. APIs time out, webhooks arrive late, and platforms have maintenance windows. The automation needs a defined recovery behavior.

Retries should be limited and spaced appropriately so they do not overwhelm a system already under stress. More importantly, retrying must not create duplicates. If the first attempt created a sales order but failed before recording success, the second attempt must recognize that order rather than creating another one.

This concept is often called idempotency. Operations teams do not need to use the term daily, but they feel the consequences when duplicate invoices, contacts, tasks, or fulfillment requests appear. Test it directly by forcing an interruption after a downstream action has completed.

6. Check security and access boundaries

Test using the same user roles, service accounts, and permission scopes that will run in production. A workflow that works under an administrator account may fail when deployed with appropriately restricted credentials.

Confirm that the integration accesses only what it needs and that sensitive data is not exposed in logs, notifications, or error messages. This deserves extra attention for employee records, payment data, healthcare information, contracts, and customer financial details.

Also define credential ownership. If an employee's personal account owns the connection, the automation can fail the day that employee leaves. Production systems should use managed credentials with a documented renewal process.

7. Test monitoring before the launch date

Monitoring is not a feature to add later. Confirm that your team can see transaction volume, success rates, failure rates, queue depth, and unresolved exceptions. A workflow that is silently failing is worse than a manual process because people continue to trust data that is no longer current.

Set thresholds based on the business impact. A failed daily report may be tolerable for a few hours. A failed order-routing workflow may need an immediate alert and manual fallback. The response plan should match the cost of delay.

Define Acceptance Criteria That Operations Can Sign Off On

Technical teams should not be the only people approving an integration. The owner of the workflow should review test results against agreed acceptance criteria. This keeps the project focused on the business result rather than a vague statement that the integration is "working."

Acceptance criteria might include: all required customer fields reach the ERP correctly; duplicate records are prevented; failed transactions are retried and surfaced within five minutes; an operations manager can resolve a standard exception without developer involvement; and month-end volume completes within an agreed window.

The right criteria depend on the process. Finance workflows typically require tighter controls and audit trails. Sales workflows may prioritize speed and clean routing. Customer support workflows may prioritize accurate context and clear ownership. There is no universal test suite, but there should always be a clear definition of acceptable performance.

Avoid the Common Shortcut: Testing Only the Happy Path

Teams often rush testing because the visible automation is already functioning. That is understandable, especially when manual work is expensive and the pressure to launch is real. But the cost of a short test cycle shows up later as rework, broken trust, and staff returning to spreadsheets to verify every automated action.

The better approach is targeted rigor. Put the most testing effort where an error has the highest cost: financial transactions, customer commitments, inventory, compliance data, and workflows with multiple downstream dependencies. A lower-risk internal notification can move faster. An automation that changes revenue records should be tested like the operational system it is.

Treat Testing as Part of the System You Buy

A custom integration is not finished when records begin moving. It is finished when the business can depend on it, diagnose it, and recover from predictable failures without a scramble. That is why Intellismart Group builds error handling, monitoring, and post-launch support into automation work rather than treating them as optional extras.

Before approving your next automation release, ask for the test evidence, the exception process, and the monitoring plan. If those answers are unclear, the system is not ready for production, no matter how good the demo looks.
