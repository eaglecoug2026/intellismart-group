How to Map Approval Bottlenecks Before They Cost You

Learn how to map approval bottlenecks, measure their cost, find decision delays, and build fixes that keep orders, invoices, and projects moving daily.

An invoice can be entered in two minutes and still sit for 12 days waiting for approval. A sales discount can be justified, reviewed, and lost before anyone answers. These are not minor administrative annoyances. They are operational delays with a dollar value. Knowing how to map approval bottlenecks gives you the evidence to fix the real constraint instead of buying another tool or blaming the people closest to the work.

For most businesses, the approval process is not documented as one clean workflow. It is a mixture of ERP rules, email threads, Slack messages, spreadsheets, exceptions, and institutional knowledge. Work moves quickly until it reaches the one decision that requires a particular person, a missing piece of information, or a system that does not notify anyone correctly.

The goal is not to remove every approval. Some controls protect cash, margin, compliance, and customer relationships. The goal is to identify where approvals are adding risk reduction and where they are simply adding waiting time.

Start With One Expensive Approval Flow

Do not begin by mapping every approval in the company. That creates a large documentation project and very little action. Start with a workflow tied to a measurable business outcome: accounts payable invoices, purchase orders, customer credits, contract redlines, pricing exceptions, expense reports, new vendor setup, or order releases.

Choose the process where delays create visible cost. Finance leaders may start with invoices that miss early-payment discounts or create late fees. Revenue operations teams may focus on discount approvals that slow deals at the end of the quarter. Operations teams may choose purchase approvals that hold up production or field work.

Define the start and finish in plain language. For example, an invoice workflow may begin when a vendor invoice arrives and end when it is approved for payment. A quote approval may begin when a rep submits a nonstandard price and end when the customer receives the approved proposal. Without a clear boundary, teams tend to include unrelated work and hide the actual delay.

How to Map Approval Bottlenecks Step by Step

A useful map follows the work, not the org chart. Capture what happens in reality, including the unofficial shortcuts people use to get urgent items moving.

Record every handoff and decision

For each stage, document the trigger, the person or role responsible, the system used, the information required, and the next action. Include automated actions as well as human decisions. An approval might be assigned in NetSuite, but the approver may receive the actual request by email, ask for context in Slack, and enter the decision later in the ERP.

That distinction matters. If the workflow map says "manager approves in NetSuite" but the manager never opens NetSuite until someone sends a reminder, the bottleneck is not necessarily the approval policy. It may be a notification failure or a poor approval interface.

Ask the people doing the work to walk through a recent real example. Avoid relying solely on written procedures. Employees generally know where a request gets stuck, what information approvers repeatedly ask for, and which exceptions force them to restart the process.

Separate touch time from wait time

Every step has two clocks. Touch time is the actual labor required to review, correct, or approve something. Wait time is the period when no one is actively working on it.

A manager may spend three minutes approving a purchase request that waited 36 hours in a queue. If you only measure labor time, the process looks efficient. If you measure elapsed time, you see the operational problem.

Capture timestamps for submission, assignment, first view, request for information, resubmission, approval, rejection, and final completion. If your systems do not record these events cleanly, pull samples from email, chat history, ERP audit logs, and spreadsheets. Even two to four weeks of data can reveal a pattern worth fixing.

Map the exception paths

The straight path is rarely where work stalls. Bottlenecks often appear when the vendor name does not match the purchase order, a customer wants terms outside policy, a department head is traveling, or a request exceeds a threshold by a few dollars.

Map what happens after a request is rejected, returned for missing information, reassigned, escalated, or approved conditionally. Measure how often each exception occurs and how long it adds. A process with a 90 percent first-pass approval rate has a very different problem from one where half of requests are returned for basic missing fields.

Measure the Cost of the Delay

A bottleneck is not simply the stage with the longest average time. A five-day legal review may be appropriate for high-risk contracts. A four-hour wait on a low-value office supply order may not matter. Prioritize the delays that affect cash, revenue, service levels, or management capacity.

Look at median approval time as well as the average. Averages can hide a small number of requests that sit for weeks. Also compare approval time by approver, department, request type, value range, day of week, and system source. The pattern may show that requests submitted after Thursday afternoon consistently roll into the following week, or that one category requires manual data gathering before anyone can decide.

Translate delay into business terms. The math may include missed payment discounts, delayed billing, sales opportunities that expire, expedited shipping, idle labor, or the time employees spend chasing approvals. If five coordinators spend 30 minutes each day sending reminders and checking status, that is not free. It is recurring labor spent compensating for a broken workflow.

You should also measure rework. A request that is returned twice may be more costly than a request that waits a day for a qualified approver. Rework usually signals unclear policy, incomplete intake data, or disconnected systems.

Find the Cause, Not Just the Slow Person

It is tempting to identify a slow approver and call the analysis finished. Sometimes a specific role is overloaded, but the root cause is usually more structural.

Common causes include approval thresholds that no longer match the business, unclear authority rules, duplicate approvals across departments, missing data at intake, requests routed to inactive employees, and systems that do not show a decision-maker the context needed to act. Another frequent issue is serial routing. A request may pass through four people in sequence even though three could review it at the same time.

Ask a practical question at each delay: what must be true for this person to make a safe decision? If the approver needs budget data, customer history, contract terms, or inventory status, the right fix may be to present that information automatically in the approval request. Requiring people to hunt through Salesforce, QuickBooks, NetSuite, and email creates delay even when everyone is responsive.

There is a trade-off. Automatically approving more requests can improve cycle time but increase financial or compliance exposure. The better approach is usually risk-based routing: automate low-risk, policy-compliant work; send higher-value or unusual cases to the right reviewer; and preserve a complete audit trail.

Redesign the Workflow Around Decisions

Once the map shows where work waits, simplify the route before adding automation. Remove approvals that are informational rather than decision-making. Set clear dollar thresholds. Assign backup approvers. Route independent reviews in parallel. Standardize what a complete request looks like.

Then automate the administrative work around the decision. A well-built workflow can validate required fields before submission, pull supporting data from connected systems, assign the request based on value or department, notify the right person, escalate before service-level deadlines are missed, and write the decision back to the source system.

For example, an accounts payable workflow can match invoice data against purchase orders and receipts, route only exceptions for review, and send approvers a compact summary with the documents and coding they need. A sales approval workflow can calculate margin from CRM and ERP data, apply pricing rules, and send only nonstandard deals to a commercial leader. The approver still owns the decision. The system removes the scavenger hunt.

Build error handling into the design. A request should not disappear because an employee changed roles, an integration credential expired, or a source record was missing a field. Production-grade automation needs monitoring, alerts, retry logic where appropriate, and a clear process for resolving exceptions. Otherwise, you have simply moved the bottleneck somewhere harder to see.

Validate the Fix With Operating Metrics

Do not declare victory at launch. Set a baseline before changes and track the same measures afterward: median cycle time, percentage completed within the target window, first-pass approval rate, number of reminders, exception volume, and labor spent on follow-up.

Watch for unintended effects. If approval time falls but exception volume rises, the intake rules may be too loose. If approvals are faster but finance has more coding corrections later, the workflow may have removed a necessary check. Good automation makes controls clearer and easier to operate, not invisible.

The best approval maps become working management tools, not diagrams saved in a shared folder. They show where money and time are waiting, who has the information to act, and which rules deserve automation.
